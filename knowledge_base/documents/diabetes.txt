Diabetes

Diabetes is a long-term condition where the amount of sugar (glucose) in the blood stays too high. Normally, a hormone called insulin, made by the pancreas, helps move sugar from the blood into the body's cells to be used as energy. In diabetes, the body either doesn't make enough insulin or can't use it properly, so sugar builds up in the blood instead.

There are different types. Type 1 usually starts in childhood and means the body makes little or no insulin. Type 2 is far more common in adults and is linked to being overweight, low physical activity, and family history. Gestational diabetes can appear during pregnancy and is usually checked for between the 24th and 28th week.

Common symptoms include feeling very thirsty often, needing to urinate frequently, feeling unusually tired, blurred vision, slow-healing cuts or wounds, and unexplained weight loss. Some people, especially early on, have no noticeable symptoms at all, which is why regular check-ups matter if diabetes runs in the family.

Diabetes cannot be cured, but it can be managed well. For type 2 diabetes, lifestyle changes make a real difference: eating smaller portions with more vegetables and whole grains and less sugar and fried food, staying physically active most days (even brisk walking helps), and working toward a healthy body weight. Some people also need medicine or insulin, which a doctor will prescribe and adjust based on regular blood sugar testing.

Left unmanaged over many years, high blood sugar can damage the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart, and blood vessels, and can slow healing of wounds on the feet.

When to see a doctor: if someone has the symptoms above, especially constant thirst and frequent urination; if a person already diagnosed with diabetes has very high or very low blood sugar symptoms (such as confusion, shaking, sweating, or fainting); if a wound, especially on the foot, is not healing or looks infected; or for anyone with a family history of diabetes who wants to get tested. This information is for general education only and does not replace testing or advice from a qualified health worker.
