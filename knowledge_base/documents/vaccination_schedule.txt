Childhood Vaccination

Vaccination protects children from serious diseases by training the body's immune system to recognize and fight specific infections before the child is ever exposed to the real disease. This is one of the most effective ways to prevent serious childhood illness, disability, and death, and it also helps protect the wider community by reducing the spread of disease.

In general, routine childhood vaccination schedules cover diseases such as tuberculosis, polio, diphtheria, pertussis (whooping cough), tetanus, hepatitis B, measles, and others, given at specific ages from birth through the first few years of life, often through programs like Pakistan's Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), which provides these vaccines free at government health facilities.

It's normal for a baby to have mild side effects after a vaccine, such as slight fever, fussiness, or soreness/swelling where the injection was given. These usually go away within a day or two and are a sign the immune system is responding, not a sign something is wrong.

Because exact vaccines, doses, and timing depend on the child's age and the specific national schedule (which can be updated), a parent should follow the schedule given by a health worker, EPI center, or vaccination card rather than relying on general information for exact dosing or timing. Keeping the vaccination card safe and bringing it to every visit helps the health worker track what's due next.

Missing a scheduled vaccine is not a reason to give up on it — it's usually possible to catch up, so a parent should visit the nearest health facility as soon as possible rather than skipping it entirely.

When to seek medical advice: if a baby has a very high fever, seems unusually unwell, has a severe allergic reaction (difficulty breathing, swelling of the face/lips, widespread rash) after a vaccine, or if a parent is unsure about the vaccination schedule or a missed dose. This information is general education only; always follow the specific schedule and guidance from a qualified health worker or EPI center.
