Fever in a Child

Fever means the body's temperature is higher than normal, usually above 100.4°F (38°C). It is not an illness by itself — it's a sign that the body's immune system is responding to an infection, which is most often a common virus and not something serious.

In young children, fever can look alarming because it often comes with fussiness, reduced appetite, or tiredness. Most fevers in children are caused by ordinary infections like colds, throat infections, or stomach bugs, and settle on their own within a few days.

Safe home care while watching a feverish child: dress them in light clothing, keep the room comfortably cool (not cold), offer plenty of fluids to prevent dehydration (water, breast milk, or oral rehydration solution for babies), and use a paracetamol-based children's fever medicine at the correct weight-based dose if the child is uncomfortable — never use adult medicine or aspirin for children. A lukewarm sponge bath can help comfort a child but should not involve cold water or ice. Let the child rest; forcing food is not necessary if fever reduces appetite, but keep offering fluids regularly.

Warning signs that need urgent medical care: fever in a baby under 3 months old (any fever at this age needs prompt medical attention); fever above 104°F (40°C); the child is difficult to wake, unusually drowsy, or not responding normally; signs of dehydration such as no urination for 8+ hours, dry mouth, or no tears when crying; a rash that doesn't fade when pressed; difficulty breathing, fast breathing, or persistent vomiting; a seizure or fit; stiff neck or the child crying inconsolably; or fever lasting more than 3 days without improvement.

If a rural clinic or hospital is not immediately reachable and any of the warning signs above appear, treat it as an emergency and seek the fastest possible transport to medical care. This information is educational only and is not a substitute for a doctor's examination of the child.
