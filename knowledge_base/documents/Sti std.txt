Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs)
 
Sexually transmitted infections, sometimes called STDs (sexually transmitted diseases), are infections that spread from one person to another through sexual contact. They can be caused by bacteria, viruses, or parasites. Anyone who is sexually active can get an STI, regardless of age, marital status, or background — it is a medical topic, not a moral one, and getting informed or tested is a normal part of taking care of one's health.
 
Many STIs cause no symptoms at all, especially early on, which is why someone can have an infection and not know it, and can still pass it to a partner without realizing it. When symptoms do occur, they can include unusual discharge, burning or pain during urination, itching, sores, bumps, or rashes in the genital area, and sometimes fever or lower abdominal pain. Because these symptoms can look similar across different infections — and because some infections cause no symptoms at all — a proper diagnosis requires testing at a clinic or hospital, not guesswork based on symptoms alone.
 
The most reliable way to avoid STIs entirely is not having vaginal, anal, or oral sex. For those who are sexually active, the risk can be significantly lowered through several practical steps: using condoms correctly and consistently during every sexual encounter; limiting the number of sexual partners; having open, honest conversations with a partner about sexual health and testing before becoming sexually active together; and avoiding sharing needles or other items that come into contact with blood. Some specific infections, such as hepatitis B and certain strains of HPV (human papillomavirus), can also be prevented through vaccination, which may be available through local health facilities.
 
Most STIs are treatable, and many are fully curable, especially when caught early — bacterial infections are generally treated with antibiotics, while viral infections may be managed with other medicines depending on the specific condition. Left untreated, some STIs can lead to more serious long-term health problems, including effects on fertility, so timely testing and treatment matter even when symptoms are mild or absent.
 
Testing is simple, usually quick, and increasingly available at government and private health facilities. There is no way to reliably tell whether someone has an STI just by how they look or feel, so testing is the only way to know for certain — a person does not need to feel unwell or have visible symptoms to have a reason to get tested, particularly after a new sexual partner or unprotected contact.
 
When to see a doctor or health worker: any unusual discharge, sores, itching, burning during urination, or pain in the genital area; if a sexual partner has been diagnosed with an STI, even without symptoms; before starting a sexual relationship with a new partner, as part of routine health care; or during pregnancy, since some infections can affect the health of the baby if untreated, and testing is often recommended as part of routine antenatal care. This information is educational only and does not replace testing, diagnosis, or treatment from a qualified health worker — conversations about sexual health can be sensitive, but health workers are trained to discuss them respectfully and confidentially.
