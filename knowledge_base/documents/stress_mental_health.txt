Stress and Mental Health

Feeling stressed, worried, or emotionally overwhelmed sometimes is a normal part of life, especially during difficult times like financial hardship, illness in the family, or heavy workloads. Stress becomes something to pay closer attention to when it's constant, affects sleep and appetite over time, or makes it hard to manage daily responsibilities.

Common signs of ongoing stress include trouble sleeping or sleeping too much, low energy or motivation, irritability, difficulty concentrating, physical symptoms like headaches or stomach discomfort without a clear physical cause, and feeling persistently sad, anxious, or on edge.

Everyday things that can genuinely help: keeping a regular sleep routine as much as possible, even brief physical activity like walking, talking with a trusted family member or friend rather than carrying worries alone, taking short breaks during demanding days, limiting excessive worry-inducing news or content when it's not helpful, and practicing simple breathing or relaxation for a few minutes when feeling overwhelmed. None of these are cures for serious mental health conditions, but they can genuinely ease day-to-day stress for many people.

There is no shame in struggling with stress, anxiety, or low mood — these are common and treatable, not a sign of weakness or something to hide. Talking to a health worker about how you're feeling is a reasonable and healthy step, the same as it would be for a physical symptom.

When to seek support from a health worker or counselor: stress or low mood that lasts for weeks and doesn't improve, significant changes in sleep or appetite, withdrawing from family and normal activities, feeling unable to cope with daily life, or physical symptoms (chest tightness, persistent headaches) linked to stress that don't go away. If a person ever has thoughts of harming themselves or feels life isn't worth living, this needs urgent attention — reaching out to a trusted person, health worker, or local emergency service right away is important, and they should not be left alone. This information is educational only and is not a substitute for professional mental health support.
