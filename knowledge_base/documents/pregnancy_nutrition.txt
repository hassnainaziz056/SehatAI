Pregnancy Nutrition

Good nutrition during pregnancy supports both the mother's health and the baby's growth. It doesn't require expensive or special foods — mostly it means eating a varied, balanced diet a little more than usual and paying attention to a few key nutrients.

Helpful, locally affordable foods: lentils (daal), beans, and chickpeas for protein and iron; leafy green vegetables like spinach (saag) for iron and folate; eggs, milk, and yogurt for protein and calcium; seasonal fruits for vitamins; whole grains like whole wheat roti and brown rice for energy and fiber; and small amounts of nuts if available. Iron-rich foods are especially important because pregnancy increases the body's need for iron, and many women in rural areas are already low in iron. Folic acid (found in leafy greens, lentils, and often given as a supplement) is important, particularly in early pregnancy, to support the baby's development.

Many health programs provide free iron and folic acid tablets during pregnancy — these are usually recommended in addition to diet, not instead of it, so it's worth asking a health worker if this pregnant woman is receiving them.

Foods and habits to avoid or limit: raw or undercooked meat and eggs, unpasteurized (open, unboiled) milk, excess tea or coffee close to meals (it can reduce iron absorption), alcohol, and tobacco or paan/gutka use. Very heavy, spicy, or oily food in large amounts can also worsen nausea or discomfort for some women.

Eating smaller, more frequent meals can help with nausea, especially in early pregnancy. Drinking enough water throughout the day is also important.

Danger signs that need urgent medical attention regardless of the stage of pregnancy: heavy vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, severe headache with blurred vision or swelling of the face/hands (possible signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy), high fever, reduced or no baby movement after the point movements are normally felt, fluid leaking from the vagina before due date, or fainting. This information is educational only; every pregnant woman should have regular antenatal check-ups with a qualified health worker.
